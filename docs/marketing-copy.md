## Verified stats (safe to cite anywhere)

Always defensible. Pair with the citation if the audience is critical (Reddit, blog comments, anywhere a smart prospect might Google).

- **After three days, plain text recall drops to ~10%. With image + text it stays at ~65%.** — Lester (2006), Pictorial Superiority Effect
- **Pictures are encoded into both visual *and* semantic memory pathways automatically; words alone trigger only one.** — Paivio (1971), Dual Coding Theory
- **Pictures and words are recalled at roughly 6× the rate of words alone in standard recall studies.** — Pictorial Superiority Effect, multiple replications
- **Visuals are processed by the brain ~60,000× faster than text.** — 3M Corp study (often cited)

The Robert E. Horn / Stanford quote (worth using verbatim for credibility on long-form content):

> "When words and visual elements are closely entwined, we create something new and we augment our communal intelligence ... visual language has the potential for increasing 'human bandwidth' — the capacity to take in, comprehend, and more efficiently synthesize large amounts of new information."
> — *Robert E. Horn, Stanford University*

## Unverified stats — DO NOT USE without finding a real source

These showed up in early drafts but I couldn't verify them. Using made-up numbers gets exposed by anyone who Googles. Listed here so we don't accidentally re-introduce them.

- ❌ "It takes the average person 20 times seeing a card to remember it. With pictures it drops to 4-6."
- ❌ "Visuals improve learning by 400%."
- ❌ "The brain processes visual information X times faster than text" — only safe with the 3M Corp 60,000× figure above; other versions float around with weak provenance.

If we want to use the "X views to remember" angle, we have to find a real published study or rephrase as defensible (e.g. "studies suggest pictures dramatically reduce the number of repetitions needed to commit a word to memory" — vague but honest).

## Long-form science passage

For blog posts, founder posts, or "the science" section of the landing page:

> The reason most flashcard apps fail to deliver on memory isn't the algorithm — Anki's spaced repetition is sound. It's the format.
>
> Words are processed through the semantic pathway: meaning, abstract concept, label. Pictures are processed through *both* the visual pathway and the semantic pathway. This is dual-coding theory (Paivio, 1971) — one of the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology. The Pictorial Superiority Effect, demonstrated repeatedly since Lester's 2006 work, shows the practical consequence: after three days, recall of plain text drops to around 10%, while recall of paired image + text stays around 65%.
>
> The practical implication for flashcards: you're either making your brain do the visualization work itself (which is exhausting and uneven), or you're skipping the visual encoding entirely and wondering why the same word keeps coming back as "Again" night after night.
>
> FlashMind generates the illustration for you, automatically, on every card. Same algorithm, same study time. Different memory.
